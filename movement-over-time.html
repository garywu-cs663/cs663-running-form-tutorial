Section 2

Understanding Athletic Movement Over Time

Running is a repeating cycle of phases that always happen in the same order. This page breaks down that cycle and shows why its timing matters for computer vision.

This section looks at running as a movement that unfolds over time. A running stride repeats a set of phases: initial contact, when the foot lands; stance, when the body passes over the foot; push-off, when the foot leaves the ground; and swing, when the leg recovers forward. Running also has flight periods, when neither foot is touching the ground.

These phases always happen in the same order, and their timing changes with speed and from runner to runner. Two runners can look nearly identical in one frame but move very differently across the whole stride. For example, one might stay on the ground much longer than the other.

Computer-vision systems handle this by labeling every moment of the video with a phase and finding the boundaries where one phase turns into the next. Try the interactive timeline on this page. Move the slider through a stride and compare what one frame shows with what the full sequence reveals.

The phases of a running stride

A stride is one full cycle of a single leg, from when one foot touches the ground until that same foot lands again. It starts with initial contact, the brief moment the foot lands. Next is stance, where the body absorbs the landing and moves over the foot. Stance ends with push-off, when the leg extends and the foot leaves the ground. Finally, in swing, the leg travels forward for the next contact. Part of swing overlaps with flight, when neither foot is touching the ground. Flight is one of the clearest differences between running and walking, since walking always has a moment with both feet down.

How timing and order affect analysis

Phases give a model two kinds of information it can't get from a single frame. The first is duration, how long each phase lasts compared to the rest of the stride. Changes in speed or fatigue usually show up here first. The second is order. Because the phases always happen in the same sequence, a model can use that order as a rule. If it predicts push-off right after contact with no stance in between, something has gone wrong.

Research in other sports uses the same idea. Moodley and van der Haar split every cricket stroke into buildup, execution, and follow-through, and Han et al. divide each dive into ordered stages and model how one affects the next. Running fits this structure well and has one extra advantage: the cycle repeats, so one video can contain dozens of strides to compare [3], [4].

Here is a concrete example. Imagine two runners photographed at midstance with nearly identical posture. Over the full stride, though, they differ:

A frame-based model would describe both runners the same way, while a temporal model can tell them apart. Yin et al. point out the same problem more broadly: one action can be performed in many styles and at different speeds, which makes it hard to model with a single template [1].

Finding phases in a video

Before a system can use phases, it has to find them. The model gives every frame a probability for each phase, some models also predict where boundaries fall, and the raw labels are cleaned up into segments with start and end times. On a well-filmed diving benchmark, this works very well, with an average overlap with the true stages of about 0.99. Once a running video is split this way, a system can measure each phase, compare strides, and tie feedback to a moment, like "the left push-off was slower than the right." Section 3 explains how the models do this, and Section 6 explains why the diving result may not carry over to everyday running video [4].
