What this tutorial is about

Most people's first idea of "computer vision for sports" is a system that finds body positions in a photo. That is useful, but movement quality lives in time: the order of events, how long each one lasts, and how smoothly one phase turns into the next. Over the past decade, a research area called action-quality assessment (AQA) has grown around the question of how well an action is performed, not just which action it is [1]. This tutorial uses running form as the motivating example and focuses on the temporal methods behind that research.

Research question

How can computer-vision systems use the timing, order, and phases of movement to evaluate running form more effectively than analyzing individual video frames?

Main thesis

Recent computer-vision research is moving beyond simple frame-by-frame body-position analysis by using temporal models and action-quality assessment to understand how movements develop over time. These methods may improve running-form analysis, but they still face challenges involving limited datasets, subjective definitions of good form, different camera angles, generalization to new athletes, and explaining their feedback.

Learning objectives

By the end of this tutorial, you should be able to:

  1. Explain why a single frame cannot fully describe a running movement, and what timing and order add.
  2. Distinguish action recognition from action-quality assessment.
  3. Describe temporal action segmentation and how it finds the beginnings, transitions, and endings of movement phases.
  4. Compare temporal convolutional networks (TCNs), LSTMs, and video transformers, including their trade-offs.
  5. Walk through a video-to-quality-score pipeline and state its assumptions and operating conditions.
  6. Summarize what recent research (2025–2026) has achieved and why those results do not yet prove that running-form analysis is solved.
  7. Identify the main practical limitations, including camera angle, data size, and subjective labels.
Scope note

This is a tutorial about research methods for temporal video understanding. It does not review software libraries or pose-estimation products, and it does not cover force, pressure, or balance estimation. When a paper uses body keypoints, I mention them only as one possible input representation. Nothing here is medical advice, and no system described here diagnoses injuries.
