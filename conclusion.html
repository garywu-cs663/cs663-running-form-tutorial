Future Applications and Conclusion

In this final section I look ahead and then return to my thesis. Temporal methods could support personalized running feedback, coaching, athlete training, and, with a professional involved, rehabilitation support. Getting there will take more diverse datasets, systems that explain their feedback, faster processing, and possibly combining video with other signals such as audio.

My conclusion is that current systems can split structured movements into phases and predict scores that agree closely with expert judges on benchmark datasets. What they can't do yet is give reliable running-form feedback for any runner, from any camera, without carefully defined labels. Temporal understanding is a real step forward, but it is not a finished solution.

Future applications

The most obvious use is personalized running feedback. A runner records a short video, the system splits it into strides and phases, and it points out where one side or one phase looks different. Because running repeats, a system could compare a runner against their own earlier strides instead of one "ideal" runner. Given how subjective good form is, I think that comparison is easier to defend than a universal standard.

For coaching, the realistic near-term role is a review tool that finds the strides worth looking at and leaves the judgment to the coach. That plays to what the models are good at (segmentation and consistent measurement) and avoids what they are weak at (deciding what "good" means). Rehabilitation support is another direction the survey mentions, but here I want to be careful: a system could help a physical therapist track movement over time, but it should not diagnose anything, and none of these papers validate their methods for medical use. Real-time feedback during a run is further off, since the most accurate models are still heavy [1].

What the research needs next

The first need is more diverse datasets: recreational and elite runners, different ages, bodies, speeds, surfaces, and camera angles, with poor strides labeled on purpose so they don't get overestimated. The second is more interpretable systems. Yin et al. point to causal reasoning as one route, and FineCausal is an early example. For running, that would mean feedback naming a phase, a body region, and a confidence level. The third is combining visual, audio, and temporal information. Multimodal AQA is one of the main trends in the survey. Footstep sounds might help find contact moments when the foot is hidden, although that's my own suggestion and none of the reviewed papers test it [1], [3], [4].

Conclusion

At the start of this tutorial I asked how computer-vision systems can use the timing, order, and phases of movement to evaluate running form better than analyzing individual frames. Based on the papers I reviewed, the answer has three parts. Temporal models give each moment context from the moments around it. Segmenting a movement into ordered phases lets a system measure timing and judge each phase separately. And fine-grained action-quality assessment turns those phases into scores and, increasingly, into feedback about where and when a movement went wrong.

That supports my thesis, but only partly. On structured, well-filmed diving and cricket benchmarks, current systems segment phases very accurately and rank performances in close agreement with expert judges. But no method reviewed here has been shown to work on running video, from any camera angle, for runners unlike those in its training data. Their scores are only as good as their labels, their explanations are still partial, and the most accurate models are too slow for live feedback [3], [4].

So my overall view is that temporal understanding is the right direction for running-form analysis, and it is clearly better than looking at frames one at a time. But for now it works best as a tool that helps a coach or runner see their own movement more clearly, not as a replacement for their judgment.
